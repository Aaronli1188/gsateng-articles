Universal Basic Income in the Age of Automation Whether Guaranteed Income Programs Empower Human Potential or Reduce the Incentive to Work and Innovate

In 2017, Finland launched a two-year experiment that sent shockwaves through economic circles worldwide. The government provided 2,000 unemployed citizens with €560 per month — unconditionally, no strings attached. Recipients did not lose the money if they found jobs. The results surprised many analysts: participants reported lower stress, greater well-being, and slightly higher employment rates than the control group. This experiment placed Universal Basic Income, or UBI, at the center of a debate that has only grown louder as automation threatens to reshape the global workforce.

Supporters of UBI argue that it provides a critical safety net during a period of rapid technological change. The World Economic Forum estimated that automation could displace up to 85 million jobs by 2025. Without a guaranteed income floor, workers replaced by machines may fall into poverty before they can retrain for new roles. Proponents also point out that UBI could free people to pursue creative work, volunteer activities, or entrepreneurship — contributions that traditional employment metrics often fail to capture. Stockton, California, ran its own UBI pilot between 2019 and 2021, and participants were more likely to secure full-time employment than those who received no supplement, contradicting the fear that free money kills ambition.

Critics, however, raise legitimate concerns. Funding a national UBI program is enormously expensive; some economists calculate that a modest monthly payment to all American adults would cost over two trillion dollars annually. There is also the question of whether unconditional income could reduce the urgency to develop new skills or start businesses, potentially slowing innovation over the long term. A further concern is that UBI might replace targeted welfare programs rather than supplement them, leaving the most vulnerable citizens with less specialized support than before.

The debate ultimately hinges on a condition that no single pilot study can fully resolve: what happens when UBI is implemented nationwide, permanently, and across every income level simultaneously. Finland's experiment lasted only two years and covered a narrow demographic. Whether the psychological and economic effects observed in small trials would scale up — or break down — remains an open question that economists, governments, and citizens are still working to answer.

Ibn Battuta How a Young Moroccan Scholar Left Home for a Routine Pilgrimage and Ended Up Traveling Farther Than Any Person in the Premodern World

By the time Ibn Battuta finally returned to Morocco in 1354, he had covered an estimated 75,000 miles across roughly 44 modern countries — a distance no other traveler in the premodern world is known to have matched. Marco Polo, often celebrated as history's greatest explorer, traveled far less ground, making Ibn Battuta's achievement all the more remarkable given how little attention he receives in Western textbooks.

The journey began modestly. In 1325, a 21-year-old legal scholar from Tangier set out for Mecca to perform the hajj, the Islamic pilgrimage that every Muslim is expected to complete at least once. It was a standard religious duty, not an expedition. He traveled alone, without the backing of a king or a wealthy institution, and he later admitted that the separation from his family left him in tears before he had even crossed into Algeria. Few who saw him leave could have predicted that he would not settle permanently in Morocco for nearly three decades.

What kept him moving was a combination of curiosity, ambition, and opportunity. After completing the hajj, Ibn Battuta found that Islamic trade networks had created a kind of connected world stretching from West Africa to Southeast Asia. Scholars and judges — Ibn Battuta's profession — were in demand across this entire region. He accepted appointments in Mali, India, and the Maldives, where he briefly served as a chief judge. Each position led to another journey, and each journey deepened his knowledge of geography, culture, and governance in ways no formal education could have provided.

His experiences were eventually recorded in a book known as the Rihla, meaning "The Journey," dictated to a scholar named Ibn Juzayy under the order of the Sultan of Morocco. Though some historians have questioned the accuracy of certain claims, the Rihla remains one of the most detailed firsthand accounts of the medieval Islamic world ever produced. It continues to serve as a primary historical source for researchers studying 14th-century societies across three continents.
