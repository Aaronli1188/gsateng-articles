A student argues that hiking Hehuan Mountain should be a required school activity

Standing at 3,416 meters above sea level, Hehuan Mountain is one of the highest peaks that students in Taiwan can reach by road. I believe every junior high school student should be required to hike it at least once.

The climb teaches something that no classroom can offer: how to keep going when your body wants to stop. Last year, a group of students from Taichung hiked the main peak trail. By the halfway point, many of them wanted to turn back. Those who finished said the experience changed how they think about their own ability. That kind of lesson is not found in any textbook.

Some teachers worry that the hike is too dangerous for students who are not in good shape. This worry is fair. High up on the mountain, students may feel dizzy or get a headache, and not everyone is ready for a long walk in cold weather. These risks should not be ignored.

Still, with careful planning and help from teachers, the climb is possible for most healthy students. Perhaps the better question is not whether it is difficult, but what we lose if students never try something that truly challenges them.

Opinion on whether schools should ban students from using phones during lunch breaks

During last Tuesday's lunch break, half the students at a nearby school were sitting alone, staring at their phones instead of talking to each other. Schools should not ban phones during lunch — but they should teach students how to use them better.

The strongest reason is that lunch is personal time, not class time. Students need a break from lessons, and checking messages or watching a short video can help them relax. For example, a student who feels stressed after a difficult math test may feel calmer after listening to music for ten minutes. Taking that small comfort away does not help anyone.

Still, there is one honest problem. Some students spend the entire break on their phones and eat almost nothing. By afternoon, they arrive at class feeling tired and hungry. A rule that limits phone use to the second half of lunch — after eating — could be a fair answer.

Even so, a total ban feels like the wrong tool. Lunch is one of the few moments in the school day when students can choose what they do. Whether a phone makes that moment better or worse is something each student probably already knows.

Why eating breakfast at convenience stores has become a habit worth keeping

At 7-Eleven at seven in the morning, a student grabs a steamed bun and a warm cup of milk tea before running to catch the bus. It takes less than two minutes. For many Taiwanese students, eating breakfast at a convenience store has become a smart daily habit — and there are good reasons to keep it.

A convenience store near a school usually opens before 6:30 a.m. and offers fresh sandwiches, rice balls, hot soup, and boiled eggs — all under fifty dollars. Students who live far from school and have little time at home can still eat a proper meal instead of skipping breakfast entirely, which doctors say damages concentration in class.

Some people argue that convenience store food is too processed and contains too much salt or sugar. That is a fair point. Not every item on the shelf is a healthy choice, and eating the same fried chicken sandwich every day is probably not ideal.

Still, a rice ball with tuna or a bowl of instant congee is far better than nothing. The question is not whether convenience store breakfast is perfect — it is whether having a regular morning meal helps students start the day better. Most would say yes.

A student's case for why handwriting is still worth practicing in the digital age

Every student in my class types notes on a tablet now, and I used to think handwriting was just something old teachers cared about. After failing two history tests in a row last semester, I changed my mind.

The strongest reason to keep practicing handwriting is that it helps you remember information. When you write by hand, your brain works harder to process each word. During exam review, I copied my notes by hand instead of reading them on a screen. The following week, I could recall dates and names much more clearly than before. Scientists have also found that students who write by hand tend to understand new ideas more deeply than those who only type.

Of course, typing is faster and easier to fix. In group projects or long reports, a keyboard clearly saves time. Nobody wants to rewrite three pages because of one mistake. This is a real advantage that is hard to ignore.

Still, speed is not the only thing that matters in learning. Handwriting slows you down in a way that can actually help your brain hold onto ideas. Whether that is worth it probably depends on how you learn best.

Opinion on whether Taiwanese students should be required to learn Hokkien in school

Every week in Taiwan, fewer young people can speak Hokkien than the week before. Because of this, many people now ask whether schools should require students to learn it. The answer, in my view, is yes — Hokkien should be a required subject, not something students can simply skip.

Language carries history. When people stop speaking Hokkien, they also lose the songs, stories, and ideas that only exist in that language. In Tainan, for example, many temple events are still held in Hokkien. Students who cannot understand the words are cut off from those moments completely. A required class would give every student at least a basic connection to this part of their culture.

Some students may argue that Hokkien lessons take time away from other subjects, and that not everyone has family members who speak it at home. This is a fair point. Learning a language without any practice outside the classroom can feel difficult and even pointless.

Still, a required class does not need to be long or hard. Even one short lesson each week would plant something in students' minds. Years later, they might hear an older relative speak and suddenly recognize a word. Whether that moment matters to them is something only they can decide.

A review of trying to learn English exclusively through K-pop lyrics for one month

After spending one month studying English only through K-pop lyrics, I have a clear opinion: it works better than most people expect, but only for certain skills. Singing along to songs is a natural way to practice, and it never feels like homework.

The biggest advantage was learning vocabulary through songs. BTS's "Dynamite" repeats words like "shine," "light," and "free" in ways that are easy to remember because the rhythm connects to the meaning. After three weeks, those words appeared naturally when I wrote in my English diary. Songs also helped me hear how words sound together, which reading a textbook alone cannot do.

However, K-pop lyrics are not always normal English. Many sentences are broken or mixed with Korean, and some grammar is changed to fit the rhythm. I searched for phrases like "I'mma light it up" in a grammar guide and found nothing useful. That kind of English sounds cool in a song but causes real confusion during a writing test.

Still, the one-month experiment gave me something no textbook ever did — a reason to listen carefully every single day. Whether that feeling is enough to build strong English skills is a question worth testing yourself.

Should schools replace one exam per semester with a creative project instead?

Last semester, a student in Taipei spent two weeks building a model city from cardboard and recycled plastic to explain traffic problems in her neighborhood. Her classmates paid attention the entire time. Schools should replace at least one exam per semester with a creative project. Tests measure what students remember, but projects show what they can do.

The strongest reason is that projects build skills that exams cannot test. When students plan and present their work, they learn to speak clearly and work as a team. A student who builds a water filter for a science class learns how materials work together — and what they learn stays with them longer than a score on a test paper.

Not every student finds this easy. Some feel nervous about presenting in front of others. Others may lack quiet space or materials at home. These are real problems, and schools would need to offer extra support to keep things fair for everyone.

Still, one project per semester is a small change that could make a real difference for students who never shine on a written test. The student with the cardboard city — did she prove something that a multiple-choice exam never could? That is worth thinking about.

A handwritten schedule for organizing a traditional New Year's Eve family dinner

Grandma Lin's schedule notebook sat open on the kitchen table, covered in careful handwriting and small drawings of dumplings. She had written it the week before Chinese New Year's Eve to make sure everyone in the family knew what to do. Every year, the dinner had twenty dishes, and this year she needed help.

Then, it showed the cooking order — Aunt Helen was in charge of a slow-cooked pork dish starting at two in the afternoon. Finally, a seating chart at the bottom showed where each person would sit at the round table.

One problem appeared on the day itself. Aunt Helen called to say she would arrive late because of a traffic jam on the highway. Grandma Lin quietly crossed out "2:00 PM" next to the pork dish and wrote "3:30 PM" in red ink. She moved two other dishes earlier to fill the gap.

By six o'clock, every dish was ready. The schedule, now covered in small changes and extra notes, was folded and placed between the sugar jar and the soy sauce bottle, where it stayed until the following year.

A chart comparing sugar content in ten popular Taiwanese drinks

A medium cup of pearl milk tea contains about 62 grams of sugar — more than two cans of cola. Most people have no idea.

A recent chart compared the sugar content in ten popular Taiwanese drinks, including pearl milk tea, green tea, lemon fruit tea, sports drinks, and soda. At the top of the list, winter melon tea had 58 grams of sugar per serving. Plain green tea, near the bottom, had only 2 grams. The gap between the sweetest and the least sweet drink was nearly 56 grams — equal to about fourteen teaspoons of sugar.

Many students pick fruit tea because the word "fruit" sounds healthy. However, the chart showed that a large lemon fruit tea actually contained more sugar than a chocolate milk shake. The extra sugar does not come from the fruit. It comes from the syrup that drink shops add during preparation, and the amount can change depending on the cup size.

In Taiwan, most drink shops let customers choose their sugar level — full sugar, half sugar, or no sugar. The chart shows that even "half sugar" in a sweet drink can still contain more sugar than a plain soda.

A fact sheet about coral reef decline along Taiwan's southern coastline

Around 90% of Taiwan's southern coral reefs have been damaged in the past fifty years. That number is hard to believe when you stand on a beach in Kenting and look at the clear blue water. Beneath the surface, however, many reefs are pale, broken, or covered in algae.

The main causes are rising water temperature and pollution from the coast. When the ocean becomes too warm, coral loses the tiny living things that give it color and energy. This process is called coral bleaching. In 2020, researchers recorded serious bleaching events along a large section of the Kenting coastline. Fishing nets and chemicals from farms near the shore also damage the reef over time.

Many people believe that bleached coral is dead, but that is not always true. A bleached reef can recover if the water cools quickly and pollution is reduced. The problem is that these two conditions are becoming harder and harder to reach at the same time.

The reefs near Kenting support hundreds of fish species, which local fishermen and divers depend on every day. If you have eaten fresh fish in a southern Taiwan restaurant, there is a good chance it came from waters close to a reef that is now under serious pressure.

Why certain foods taste blander when eaten on an airplane

Have you ever ordered tomato juice on a plane, even though you never drink it at home? Many people do. Something about flying changes the way food tastes, and there is a real reason behind it.

Inside an airplane, the air is very dry and the pressure is lower than on the ground. Both conditions affect your nose and tongue at the same time. About 80% of what we call "taste" actually comes from smell. Your nose needs water in the air to detect smells properly. When the cabin air is too dry, this ability drops by around 30%. Salt and sweet flavors become harder to notice, while sour ones stay mostly the same.

Airlines already know this, which is why airplane meals often contain more salt and sugar than the same dish made at home. Without extra seasoning, a simple soup would seem almost bland up in the air. Tomato juice, with its strong sour flavor, matches what changed taste buds can still detect — and that is why so many passengers suddenly want it.

Next time food seems flat on a flight, your tongue is not broken. Your body has quietly adjusted to a strange new environment. It makes you wonder how much of what we "taste" depends on where we are.

How urban heat islands make city neighborhoods significantly hotter than nearby countryside

On a summer afternoon in Taipei, the ground feels almost too hot to touch. The air near the buildings is heavy and warm. But someone in a small countryside town just thirty minutes away might feel much cooler at exactly the same time.

This difference has a name: the urban heat island effect. Cities are covered with roads and hard surfaces that absorb sunlight during the day and release it as heat at night. There are very few trees to cool the air. Studies have shown that city neighborhoods can be 3 to 5 degrees Celsius hotter than nearby countryside areas.

Most people think the city is hottest at noon. In fact, the gap between city and countryside temperatures is often greatest after sunset. Roads and buildings store heat during the day and keep releasing it for hours, making summer nights feel impossible without an air conditioner.

This extra heat pushes people to use more electricity to stay cool, which creates more pollution, which can make temperatures climb even further. Walking into a small park on a hot city day, though, you can feel the air change almost right away. That quick coolness makes you wonder how different the whole city might feel with a little more green.

The science behind why we feel sleepy after eating a large meal

You finish a big bowl of rice and several dishes at lunch, and suddenly your eyes feel heavy. Your body wants to rest, but class starts in ten minutes. What is happening inside you?

When you eat a large meal, your body sends more blood to your stomach to help break down the food. Less blood reaches your brain, which makes it harder to stay awake and focused. At the same time, a large amount of food — especially rice, bread, or noodles — causes your blood sugar to rise quickly. Your body then releases a chemical called insulin to control it. This process also increases a substance in the brain connected to feeling calm and sleepy.

Many people blame the turkey at holiday dinners for making them sleepy, because turkey contains something called tryptophan. However, most scientists say the real reason is simply the size of the meal, not one specific food. Eating a large amount of anything will likely cause the same effect.

This is why some cultures have a short rest period after lunch. Your body is not being lazy — it is working hard. The next time your eyes grow heavy after a meal, you might wonder: is that feeling a sign of rest, or just your stomach asking for a little more time?

What causes the distinct earthy smell that rises from pavement right after rain

After a dry afternoon, the first drops of rain hit the warm pavement — and suddenly, a rich, earthy smell rises into the air. Almost everyone knows that smell, but not many people know where it comes from.

The smell has a name: petrichor. It is caused by a tiny chemical called geosmin, which is made by bacteria that live in the soil. When rain falls, it traps small bubbles of air in the ground. These bubbles burst and shoot tiny drops of soil into the air, carrying geosmin with them. Scientists have found that humans can detect geosmin at very low levels — as low as five parts per trillion. That is a very small amount.

Many people believe the smell comes from the rain itself. In fact, clean rainwater has almost no smell at all. The true source is the ground beneath it. Dry soil contains more geosmin because the bacteria that produce it become more active in dry conditions, waiting for moisture.

The next time rain arrives after a long dry period, that familiar smell is actually a sign of life hidden in the earth — billions of tiny living things, suddenly waking up. It is strange to think that something so small can fill an entire street with its scent.
